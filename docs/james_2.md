
# Shifts in Teaching Mode and Assessment Design


!!! quote "Editor's Note"
    
    Perhaps one day AI‑enabled wearables will be banned during presentations. That kind of dystopia may arrive eventually, yet for now one fact remains decisive: even if AI can tell you exactly how to respond to a question, without genuine understanding and involvement in your work, you cannot give a passionate answer that convinces your peers and instructors.

### Changing Patterns in Student Learning  

Dr James Martin has observed a noticeable change in how students learn and perform since the introduction of generative AI. In his *Introductory Econometrics* course (ECON 3121) at CUHK in 2025, assignment averages now sit around 90-95 %, while exam results have dropped to roughly 80-85%. He attributes this gap to students using AI tools to produce near‑perfect assignment code, which they then struggle to reproduce in exam conditions without assistance.  

Dr Martin sees this as evidence of a broader shift in academic practice rather than a shortcoming. In professional environments, people often work with AI support; what matters is the ability to interpret, validate, and apply AI‑generated results accurately. Manual coding has become a smaller part of the skillset. What now matters more is the capacity to read, understand, and verify the correctness of outputs.  

???+ info "How to assess students now?"

    Given these developments, Dr Martin suggests reviewing current assessment structures. Assignments that reward standardised AI‑generated answers can disadvantage students who work independently, offering little insight into who actually understands the material. He proposes shifting focus from factual accuracy to professional judgement — assessing how well students can evaluate AI assistance and justify their reasoning.  

    One practical option is to include presentations. Students could prepare their written materials, possibly with AI support, while final assessment would rest on their ability to handle unscripted questions and explain their approaches spontaneously.  

    Drawing on his previous teaching experience at PolyU, Dr Martin notes that smaller classes of around 40 students, with assessment tasks centred on computer and coding work, more easily allow for direct questioning and project defence sessions. That structure encouraged discussion and analysis of AI-assisted outputs in real time. Transferring that model to CUHK’s 100‑student econometrics courses poses logistical challenges, yet the aim remains the same: to design assessments that test understanding and judgement rather than replicated AI output.  

He has also noticed a change in how students approach him. Direct questions about econometric techniques have declined, as students appear to seek initial help from AI systems. When they do contact him, their questions often compare his lectures with responses given by AI tools – statements such as, “You said this, but AI says that,” or “The standard answer is this, but AI disagrees.” These exchanges highlight a new pattern: students are using AI as a constant reference and must now learn to evaluate conflicting information critically.

???+ example "Example: R‑squared and SSR  "

    One frequent area of confusion involves the use of R‑squared (R²) and SSR in regression analysis. In standard econometrics, SSR refers to *Sum of Squares Regression*, representing the portion of variation in the dependent variable explained by the model. R² is expressed as SSR divided by the total sum of squares (SST).  

    In contrast, some software systems or non‑econometric contexts define SSR as *Sum of Squared Residuals*, meaning the unexplained variation (what econometrics typically calls SSE). When students ask AI to “explain R‑squared and SSR” without including course context, it returns the broader statistical definition, which appears inconsistent with the one used in class.  

    Dr Martin uses this as an example of the importance of precision when working with AI. Students must learn to state context clearly and recognise differences in terminology, or else they risk misunderstanding correct but differently framed answers.
